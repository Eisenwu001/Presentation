Solid waste is one of the most severe financial and operational crises facing Philippine cities today. Local government units spend between 20 to 50 percent of their entire annual municipal budgets on garbage services, with Manila spending over 842 million pesos annually on private hauling. Yet despite this massive budget, urban streets remain plagued by recurring trash piles because of an extreme infrastructure deficit: Manila operates only 3 Materials Recovery Facilities for 897 barangays, and public bins were removed to deter scavenging. Barangay halls are flooded with unverified complaints, while collection trucks follow blind routes with no visibility into pile volume or flood hazards. BinSnap directly solves this breakdown through three computational pillars: first, a lightweight Progressive Web App allows residents to geotag street piles in 15 seconds; second, an automated 5-layer verification pipeline filters duplicate photos and validates waste with computer vision; and third, DBSCAN spatial clustering and Google OR-Tools schedule optimal truck routes that prioritize drainage hazard zones before vehicle capacity runs out.

CORE PROBLEM:
THE PROXIMITY PARADOX IN DISPATCH
Why the closest police station by straight-line Euclidean distance takes three times longer to arrive

MATHEMATICAL FORMULATION
argmin d_E(p, u) != argmin T(p, u, t)
The unit minimizing Euclidean distance d_E is rarely the unit minimizing dynamic travel time T at epoch t due to one-way streets, bottleneck intersections, and gridlock.
SIMULATED TELEMETRY OUTPUT
SAVINGS: 13.9 MINUTES (70.2% FASTER) // TRAVEL-TIME ALLOCATION BYPASSES ARTERIAL GRIDLOCK
OPERATIONAL IMPLICATION
Traditional CAD systems dispatch Station 1 blindly because it is 1.2 km away. Beacon calculates live route impedance, dispatching Station 3 via bypass corridor to save 13.9 minutes.
This severe congestion creates what we call the Proximity Paradox: the closest patrol car on the map is often not the fastest car to reach the crime scene. > Look at this real simulation along Quezon Boulevard. > An incident occurs. But it can take an open arterial bypass and arrive in under 6 minutes. > If the dispatcher chooses based on distance, they send the wrong car and lose 14 minutes. Beacon prevents that.

RESEARCH GAP: FEW SYSTEMS EVALUATE DYNAMIC DISPATCH CONVERGENCE IN DEVELOPING SOUTHEAST ASIAN URBAN CORRIDORS.
When we surveyed existing emergency response software, we found a clear division in literature and practice. > Most public safety systems exist in two isolated silos. > On one side, you have citizen reporting apps. They let citizens send complaints, but they stop at intake and do nothing to dispatch vehicles. > On the other side, you have static dispatch systems. They assign vehicles based on fixed precinct borders or simple map distance. > Beacon unites both worlds into a single pipeline, connecting mobile citizen reporting with dynamic, traffic-aware patrol allocation.
INVESTIGATIVE SCOPE:
RESEARCH QUESTIONS & HYPOTHESES
Core investigative inquiries and testable scientific propositions
RQ1 // ESTIMATION ACCURACY
How accurately can travel-time matrices predict actual arrival times?
H1: ML-calibrated travel-time models achieve statistically lower MAE and RMSE (p < 0.05) compared to static Euclidean speed baselines.
RQ2 // ARRIVAL TIME REDUCTION
What magnitude of response time reduction is achieved?
H2: Travel-time allocation reduces simulated police response latency by at least 25% during rush-hour traffic scenarios across Manila corridors.
RQ3 // SYSTEM CONCURRENCY & LATENCY
Can real-time telemetry operate reliably under mobile cellular constraints?
H3: WebSocket telemetry updates achieve end-to-end latency below 500ms under standard 4G/LTE mobile connectivity.
RQ4 // OPERATIONAL USABILITY
How usable is the tri-interface architecture for officers and dispatchers?
H4: The prototype scores above the standard System Usability Scale (SUS) benchmark of 70.0 in controlled user evaluation trials.
Our Beacon research is guided by four specific research questions: > Research Question One: What factors—such as distance, traffic conditions, time of day, and day of the week—affect patrol vehicle travel time? > Research Question Two: How accurate is our XGBoost-based machine learning model in predicting patrol travel time? > Research Question Three: Under what specific conditions does travel-time-based allocation choose a different car than the nearest-patrol method? > And Research Question Four: How does travel-time-based allocation compare to nearest-patrol allocation in terms of predicted response time within the Central Manila Cluster of Barbosa, Sampaloc, and Sta. Cruz?
CONCEPTUAL FRAMEWORK:
INPUT – PROCESS – OUTPUT (IPO) MODEL
Spatiotemporal incident telemetry, XGBoost travel-time prediction, and cross-jurisdiction response evaluation
INPUT
Incident Data
  • Incident Type
  • Location
  • Time of Day
Travel-Related Factors
  • Road-network distance
  • Traffic conditions
  • Time of day / day of week
  • Road characteristics
Patrol Vehicle Data
  • Vehicle Location
  • Availability
  • Vehicle type
PROCESS
Machine Learning Travel-Time Prediction
  • Train model using datasets subject to availability
  • Predict travel time for each available vehicle
Patrol Allocation Approach
Proposed Approach: Travel-time-based allocation (select vehicle with lowest predicted travel time)
Baseline Approach: Nearest-patrol allocation (select geographically nearest available vehicle)
Comparison with Nearest-Patrol Allocation
  • Compare the proposed approach with the baseline approach using the evaluation measures.
OUTPUT
Evaluation Metrics
  • Mean response time
  • Median response time
  • Prediction error (MAE, RMSE, MAPE)
  • Percentage Differences
Dependent Variable – Response Time
• Time from incident report to arrival of patrol vehicle on scene
Here is our conceptual framework, structured into a clear Input, Process, and Output flow. For INPUT, we feed three categories of data: Incident Data, including incident type, location, and time of day; Travel-Related Factors, including road-network distance, traffic conditions, day of week, and road characteristics; and Patrol Vehicle Data, including vehicle location, availability, and vehicle type. For PROCESS, our pipeline performs three actions: First, Machine Learning Travel-Time Prediction, where we train our model and predict travel times for every available vehicle. Second, our Patrol Allocation Approach, comparing our proposed travel-time selection against the baseline nearest-patrol selection. Third, a direct comparison between both approaches. For OUTPUT, we evaluate performance using response time—measured from the moment an incident is reported to vehicle arrival—tracked through mean and median response times, prediction error metrics like MAE, RMSE, and MAPE, and percentage differences.

METHODOLOGY:
SIX-STAGE EXPERIMENTAL PIPELINE
Step-by-step scientific workflow from GIS geocoding to statistical hypothesis testing
STAGE 01 // GIS GEOREFERENCING
Extract OpenStreetMap Manila road graphs and pin precise GPS coordinates for all 14 MPD stations and 897 barangays.
STAGE 02 // TRAFFIC CALIBRATION
Synthesize historical TomTom speed profiles across peak and off-peak epochs into time-dependent edge traversal costs.
STAGE 03 // MATRIX ENGINE BUILD
Compile Open Source Routing Machine (OSRM) service with custom Lua profile reflecting Manila vehicle-specific routing limits.
STAGE 04 // MACHINE LEARNING ETA
Train Artificial Neural Network / XGBoost models using spatiotemporal features to refine raw travel-time predictions.
STAGE 05 // CONTROLLED SIMULATION
Simulate 100+ emergency calls evaluating Euclidean vs Travel-time dispatch under low, medium, and extreme congestion.
STAGE 06 // STATISTICAL EVALUATION
Conduct paired t-tests and Wilcoxon signed-rank tests at alpha 0.05 to formally test H1-H4 against baseline CAD metrics.
